# GS-REF-001 — Threat Pattern Analysis

A reference document. It maps the threat patterns that concentrated, visible institutions face, and points to the defenses elsewhere in the document set. It does not create new defenses; it catalogs the threats so that the operational documents can be checked against a complete list.

## 1. Purpose

Institutions that accumulate visible assets, publish consequential findings, or hold positions others want to displace face recurring attack patterns. The patterns are not novel. They do not require a conspiracy to be effective — each can be executed by ordinary actors responding to ordinary incentives. This document lists the patterns so the Guild System's defenses can be calibrated to all of them, not only the ones that happen to come to mind.

## 2. The Patterns

### 2.1 Capture Through Grants

A funder makes a large grant, then uses the relationship to influence governance, shift orientation, or demand silence on specific findings.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-005 §2.3 (Patron Drift), GS-OPS-005 §8 (anti-concentration), GS-OPS-006 §4.5 (counterparty distribution).

### 2.2 Coordinated Financial Attack

A coordinated group shorts a related entity, freezes banking access, or pressure-tests financial resilience.

**Defense:** GS-OPS-006 §4 (five distribution requirements), §5 (contingency pool), §4.2 (jurisdictional diversity).

### 2.3 Litigation Finance

A third party funds a lawsuit to exhaust a target's resources or force settlement.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-006 §5.4 (Defense Fund), §5.9 (Counsel Network), GS-CHR-001 §5.3 (mutual defense agreements).

### 2.4 Platform Deplatforming

Coordinated reporting or platform policy changes remove public presence.

**Defense:** GS-OPS-003 (mirrors, content-addressed storage), GS-CHR-001 §6.3 (transparency reports).

### 2.5 State-Sponsored Disinformation

Coordinated campaigns to discredit findings or individuals.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-001 §6 (right of reply, transparency reports), GS-CHR-006 §8.4 (Publicize determination), signed artifacts and provenance chains (GS-CHR-009 §2).

### 2.6 Targeted Sanctions Designation

A state designates a guild or member under a sanctions regime to freeze assets and prohibit dealings.

**Defense:** GS-OPS-005 §6 (disclosure boundary), GS-OPS-006 §7 (compliance), GS-CHR-006 §6 (Legitimacy Test), lawful challenge through §8.3 (Challenge) or §8.4 (Publicize).

### 2.7 Institutional Capture Through Appointments

A captured institution places aligned individuals into a guild's governance.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-005 §2.4 (Opacity Drift), GS-CHR-006 §12 (conflict of interest), GS-FND-002 §7.3 (bounded authority), GS-CHR-010 §2.5 (term limits).

### 2.8 Legal Harassment at Scale

Dozens of small lawsuits designed to exhaust.

**Defense:** Defense Fund (GS-CHR-006 §5.4), Counsel Network, GS-CHR-007 (cross-guild dispute resolution), GS-CHR-001 §5.2 (Emergency Legal Fund).

### 2.9 Law as Weapon

A facially valid statute written to target public-interest work; retroactive liability; procedural traps; selective enforcement.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-001 §2 and §3.2 (law-as-weapon doctrine), GS-CHR-006 §6 (Legitimacy Test — a demand can satisfy local statute and fail the test), §8.4 and §8.5 (Publicize, Resist).

### 2.10 Opacity Exploitation

If a guild conceals its methods or finances, opponents use the opacity as evidence of wrongdoing. Opacity that was originally protective becomes the weapon.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-005 §2.4 (Opacity Drift), GS-OPS-005 §6 (disclosure boundary), GS-CHR-009 §2 (signed, reproducible artifacts).

### 2.11 Debtor-Creditor Inversion

A guild becomes the concentrated creditor of a sovereign or large institution. The debtor then has incentive to destroy the creditor rather than pay.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-005 §2.6 (Entanglement Drift), GS-OPS-005 §4 (counterparty rules), GS-MTX-010 (counterparty insulation matrix).

### 2.12 Irreversibility Trap

A guild cannot be forked, cannot be dissolved cleanly, cannot transfer its artifacts. Members are trapped.

**Defense:** GS-CHR-008 (Fork and Secession Protocol), GS-CHR-005 §2.7 (Irreversibility Drift), GS-CHR-010 §2.11 (fork conditions in the local charter).

## 3. What the Patterns Have in Common

Every pattern exploits one of three structural weaknesses:

1. **Concentration.** A single funder, a single jurisdiction, a single counterparty, a single point of decision. Concentration makes one attack sufficient.
2. **Visibility.** A visible target — a vault, a leader, a reputation — can be attacked. Distribution and verifiability make the target diffuse.
3. **Opacity.** Hidden methods or hidden finances look like evidence of wrongdoing when the institution is attacked. Transparency is armor, not liability.

The defenses in the operational documents address these three structural weaknesses directly. They are not pattern-specific patches; they are structural resistances that reduce the surface area all patterns can exploit.

## 4. Defensive Priorities

If the Guild System can only do three things well, they are:

1. **Distribute.** No concentration of funding, jurisdiction, counterparty, or decision authority. This defeats patterns 2.1, 2.2, 2.6, 2.11.
2. **Publish.** Signed, reproducible, mirrored artifacts. This defeats patterns 2.4, 2.5, 2.10.
3. **Guarantee exit.** Fork is always available. This defeats 2.7, 2.12.

The remaining patterns — litigation finance (2.3), legal harassment (2.8), law-as-weapon (2.9) — require active defense (Counsel Network, Defense Fund, Legitimacy Test) rather than structural prevention. They are addressed by GS-CHR-001, GS-CHR-006, and the emergency protocols.
